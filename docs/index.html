    How U.S. PVC gets priced

    1. Salt and gas, not oil. By weight PVC is roughly 57% chlorine and 43% ethylene. U.S. ethylene is cracked from ethane, a natural gas liquid, and chlorine is made by running electricity through brine. So the domestic cost floor tracks natural gas and power, not crude.
    2. Oil matters overseas, and for exports. In Europe and Asia ethylene comes from naphtha, which is refined from crude. When oil spikes, foreign PVC gets expensive, U.S. producers can export at better prices, and domestic supply tightens. That is the main way a $100 barrel reaches a pipe plant in North Carolina.
    3. Prices settle monthly, in cents per pound. Producers announce increases, large buyers push back, and the trade press reports the settlement. The Producer Price Index for thermoplastic resins is the official echo of those settlements, published mid-month for the prior month.
    4. Demand is construction. Most PVC goes into pipe, siding, windows and fittings, so housing starts, municipal water projects and interest rates move demand as much as feedstock moves cost.
    5. Expect a lag. Gas and oil react in days; resin contracts reset over one to three months. That is the reason for the shift slider above.

    Who makes North American PVC

    The market is concentrated. The largest producers are Shintech (Shin-Etsu Chemical of Japan, plants in Louisiana and Texas), Westlake (Houston), Formosa Plastics USA, OxyVinyls (Occidental Petroleum) and Orbia (formerly Mexichem, through its Vestolit business). When a few of these announce a price move or an outage, the whole market feels it.
